The Law of Cosines

A supplementary explainer for the “Generalizing beyond unit vectors: the Law of Cosines” section of Natural Language Processing: Foundations and Applications without Hallucination from Generative AI.

The Law of Cosines (also called the cosine rule) is a fact about triangles: it relates the length of one side to the other two sides and the angle between them. In the research note, it is the step that lets us go from a special case — comparing vectors of length 1 — to the general cosine similarity formula used for sentence vectors of any length. This page proves the Law of Cosines from scratch, using nothing but the Pythagorean theorem and basic right-triangle trigonometry, then shows how it leads directly to that formula.

The cosine rule

Take any triangle with sides of length a, b, and c, and let θ be the angle between sides b and c (Exhibit A). The Law of Cosines states:

a² = b² + c² − 2bc cos(θ)

Proving it: drop a perpendicular

The proof needs only one construction: from the vertex where sides a and b meet, drop a perpendicular line down onto side c (or its extension), meeting it at a point we’ll call F. This splits side c into two pieces — a piece e next to θ, and the remaining piece c − e, and creates a new segment d, the length of the perpendicular itself (Exhibit B).

Quick refresher. In a right triangle, for an angle θ: cos(θ) = adjacent ÷ hypotenuse, and sin(θ) = opposite ÷ hypotenuse.

For the triangles with three sides, e, b, d, we have:

e / b = cos(θ)  ⇒  e = b cos(θ)
d / b = sin(θ)  ⇒  d = b sin(θ)

For the triangles with three sides, (c − e), d, a, we have:

(c − e)² + d² = a²

Substituting e = b cos(θ) and d = b sin(θ):

(c − b cosθ)² + (b sinθ)² = a²
c² − 2bc cosθ + b²cos²θ + b²sin²θ = a²
c² − 2bc cosθ + b²(cos²θ + sin²θ) = a²

c² − 2bc cosθ + b² = a²
that is, a² = b² + c² − 2bc cosθ

Since cos²(θ) + sin²(θ) always equals 1 as shown below

That’s the Law of Cosines, built from nothing more than a dropped perpendicular, the definitions of sine and cosine, and the Pythagorean theorem.

From triangle sides to vectors

To use this for sentence similarity, we reinterpret b and c as vectors sharing a common starting point (the origin), rather than as plain lengths. The third side, a, becomes the vector connecting their tips: a = c − b (Exhibit C) — exactly the setup used in the research note’s own derivation.

Writing each vector by its coordinates, b = [b1, b2] and c = [c1, c2], gives:

a = c − b = [c1 − b1, c2 − b2]

Cosine similarity from the cosine rule

Now combine two ways of writing |a|². The Law of Cosines gives one:

|a|² = |b|² + |c|² − 2|b||c|cos(θ)

and the coordinates of a = c − b give another, since the squared length of a vector is just the sum of its squared coordinates:

|a|² = (c1 − b1)² + (c2 − b2)²

Setting these equal and solving for the cosine term:1

2|b||c|cosθ = |b|² + |c|² − |c − b|²
2|b||c|cosθ = (b1²+b2²) + (c1²+c2²) − [(c1−b1)² + (c2−b2)²]
2|b||c|cosθ = (b1²+b2²) + (c1²+c2²) − [(c1²−2c1b1+b1²) + (c2²−2c2b2+b2²)]
2|b||c|cosθ = 2c1b1 + 2c2b2

Dividing both sides by 2 and then by |b||c| leaves:

cos(θ) = (c1b1 + c2b2) / (|b| × |c|) = (b · c) / (|b| × |c|)

This is exactly the cosine similarity formula from the research note’s “Generalizing beyond unit vectors: the Law of Cosines” section: the dot product of two vectors, divided by the product of their lengths. Everything above followed from one construction (a dropped perpendicular) and two facts you likely already knew: the Pythagorean theorem, and what sine and cosine mean in a right triangle.
